Recent data indicate that coastal housing markets may be entering a phase of consolidation following an extended period of price growth. Prices appear to be flattening, though inventory remains limited. This pattern, however, is not consistent across regions: Sun Belt markets show early signs of rising inventory, suggesting a possible divergence in how supply constraints are resolving across different parts of the country.

A parallel shift is visible in the world of work, where the widespread adoption of remote arrangements has produced measurable changes in productivity and collaboration. In the first year following the shift, output per hour among knowledge workers rose, even as collaboration metrics showed signs of strain. This combination points to a possible tradeoff: individual efficiency gains may have come at some cost to the connective tissue of teamwork. (rewritten after critique)

These early productivity gains, however, should be interpreted with caution. It is plausible that the observed rise in output per hour partly reflects a novelty effect, an initial burst of enthusiasm or adaptation that fades as remote work becomes the norm rather than the exception. Without longitudinal data tracking whether these gains persist, regress toward baseline, or evolve over time, it would be premature to draw firm policy conclusions from a single year's results. Complicating the picture further, some of the behavioral changes accompanying remote work, such as a shift toward asynchronous documentation, appear to correlate with fewer meetings and longer, less interrupted focus blocks. While these associations are suggestive of a more deliberate, less fragmented style of work, they remain correlational rather than causal. Disentangling whether async practices drive these outcomes or simply accompany them will be essential for organizations seeking to translate early remote-work findings into durable, evidence-based policy.
